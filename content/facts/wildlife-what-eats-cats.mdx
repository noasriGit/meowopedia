## Short Answer

Several animals can prey on cats, but risk depends heavily on region and lifestyle. In many North American cities and suburbs, **coyotes** are the most documented predator of free-roaming cats.

## Common Predators by Risk Tier

| Predator | Typical risk profile |
|---|---|
| Coyotes | Most consistently documented risk in many urban/suburban areas |
| Bobcats | Regional risk, especially near wildland edges |
| Large raptors (great horned owls, large hawks) | Higher risk to kittens and very small cats |
| Foxes | Usually lower risk for healthy adult cats, higher for vulnerable cats |

## Why Some Cats Are More Vulnerable

Predation risk is not equal across all cats. Higher risk factors include:

- Kitten age or very small body size
- Illness, injury, or poor mobility
- Nighttime roaming
- Feeding stations that attract wildlife traffic
- Homes near habitat corridors (creeks, golf courses, scrub edges)

## "What Eats Cats?" vs. "What Kills Cats?"

Not every fatal wildlife interaction is predation. Some predators may attack defensively or opportunistically, and scavenging can also occur after death. That is why field confirmation can be difficult without direct observation.

## Prevention That Works

Most effective steps:

1. Keep cats indoors, especially overnight.
2. Use secure catios for outdoor enrichment.
3. Remove outdoor food sources before dusk.
4. Install motion lighting and close access points under decks and sheds.
5. Supervise high-risk cats (kittens, seniors, disabled cats) outdoors.

## Bottom Line

If your cat goes outside, predator risk is real and location-dependent. A practical safety plan should focus on coyote-aware management first, then regional predator risks such as raptors or bobcats.

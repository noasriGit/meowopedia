Cats usually get worms by swallowing parasite eggs or larvae. The most common routes are flea ingestion, hunting infected prey, and contact with contaminated feces, soil, or litter.

## How cats get worms

The highest-risk pathways are:

1. **Fleas**: Cats that swallow fleas while grooming can develop tapeworms.
2. **Hunting**: Rodents and small prey can carry larval stages of intestinal worms.
3. **Contaminated environments**: Eggs can persist in soil, boxes, litter areas, and shared outdoor spaces.
4. **Mother-to-kitten transmission**: Kittens can acquire roundworms from their mother early in life.

## Can indoor cats get worms?

Yes. Indoor status lowers risk but does not eliminate it. Fleas can enter on other pets, clothing, or household items, and parasite eggs can be tracked inside on shoes or outdoor objects.

Indoor cats are also exposed through:

- Multi-pet households
- New rescue animals
- Litter boxes with inconsistent cleaning
- Occasional access to garages, patios, or porches

## What worms look like in cats

Appearance varies by species:

- **Tapeworms**: Small white segments that look like rice grains around the rear end, in bedding, or in stool.
- **Roundworms**: Longer worms that look like pale spaghetti strands in vomit or feces.
- **Hookworms/others**: Usually not visible to the naked eye and found by fecal testing.

<VetTip title="Do not rely on appearance alone">
Many infected cats show no obvious worms. Routine fecal exams are the most reliable way to detect infections early.
</VetTip>

## Can you get worms from cats?

Some feline parasites can infect people. Risk is higher when people handle contaminated feces, garden in contaminated soil, or skip handwashing after litter box cleanup.

People at higher risk include:

- Young children
- Older adults
- Immunocompromised individuals

Basic hygiene and consistent deworming plans significantly reduce zoonotic risk.

## Tapeworms and humans

Tapeworm transmission from cats to humans is uncommon, but flea ingestion can transmit certain species. In practice, preventing fleas is one of the most effective ways to lower tapeworm risk in both cats and households.

## Diagnosis and treatment

Veterinarians diagnose worms through:

- Fecal flotation or fecal antigen testing
- Physical exam and symptom history
- Repeat tests when symptoms persist despite treatment

Treatment usually includes targeted dewormers and household control steps (especially flea treatment and litter hygiene). Not every dewormer treats every species, so diagnosis matters.

## Prevention checklist

- Keep year-round flea prevention on every pet in the home
- Scoop litter boxes daily and wash hands after cleaning
- Schedule fecal checks at routine wellness visits
- Avoid feeding raw prey or allowing hunting when possible
- Deworm kittens on veterinarian-guided schedules

If your cat has vomiting, diarrhea, weight loss, pot-belly appearance, or visible worms, book a same-week veterinary visit.

Most cats are pregnant for about **63 to 65 days**. In practical terms, that is a little over two months (about nine weeks). A normal range can run from roughly **60 to 67 days**, and some sources note wider outliers when breeding date is uncertain ([VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/pregnancy-and-parturition-in-cats), [VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/feeding-the-pregnant-cat)).

If you searched "how long are cats pregnant for" or "cat pregnancy in months," this is the answer: around 2 months, usually just over 9 weeks.

## Cat Pregnancy Timeline (Week by Week)

| Pregnancy day | What usually happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 0-14 | Conception and early embryo development; few visible signs | Keep normal routine, avoid stress, schedule a vet check if breeding was planned |
| 15-28 | Nipples may pink up and appetite may increase | Confirm pregnancy with your vet; discuss nutrition plan |
| 29-45 | Abdomen enlarges; fetal growth accelerates | Transition toward a high-quality kitten/growth diet per vet guidance |
| 46-58 | Kittens develop rapidly; queen gains weight | Set up nesting area, monitor comfort, keep environment quiet |
| 59-67 | Pre-labor signs (restlessness, nesting, appetite drop) | Observe closely, have emergency vet number ready |

<VetTip title="Why due dates can be fuzzy">
Queens are induced ovulators, so conception timing can differ from the first mating date. This is one reason estimated due dates can shift by several days.
</VetTip>

## How Many Kittens Can a Cat Have?

Litter size varies with age, genetics, breed, and overall reproductive history. Many queens have around **3 to 5 kittens**, but normal litters can be smaller or larger. Your veterinarian can often estimate litter size later in pregnancy when fetal skeletons are visible on radiographs ([VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/pregnancy-and-parturition-in-cats)).

Useful owner takeaway: the exact number matters because it helps you know when labor is likely complete.

## What Age Can Cats Get Pregnant?

Many female cats reach sexual maturity around **6 months**, but some can cycle earlier, including around **4 to 5 months** depending on season, body condition, and individual variation. That is why veterinarians commonly recommend early spay timing rather than waiting for "just one heat."

Cornell emphasizes that spaying before first heat dramatically lowers future reproductive risk and prevents pyometra (a potentially life-threatening uterine infection) ([Cornell Feline Health Center](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/spaying-and-neutering)).

## How Young or Old Is Too Young or Too Old?

### Too young

Pregnancy in a very young cat can be physically stressful because she is still growing. Young queens may have lower body reserves and more difficulty carrying and nursing kittens.

### Too old

Cats do not have a human-style menopause. Older intact queens may still cycle and can become pregnant, but risk of complications generally rises with age, especially with underlying disease or poor body condition.

## Signs Your Cat Is Nearing Labor

Common signs in the final 24 hours may include:

- Nesting behavior and seeking privacy
- Restlessness or repeated position changes
- Decreased interest in food
- More vocalization than usual
- Drop in rectal temperature in some cats (if tracked consistently)

Many cats deliver without intervention, but prepare ahead: clean nesting area, towels, and your veterinarian's emergency number.

## When to Call the Vet Urgently

<HealthAlertBox title="Call Your Veterinarian Immediately If">
- Strong contractions continue for 20 to 30 minutes with no kitten produced
- More than 2 hours pass between kittens with continued labor signs
- There is heavy bleeding, foul-smelling discharge, or collapse
- The queen is very weak, painful, or not responding normally
</HealthAlertBox>

Prompt intervention can save both the queen and kittens.

## Feeding and Care During Pregnancy

Pregnancy and lactation sharply increase energy needs. VCA guidance recommends a nutritionally complete growth/reproduction diet (often kitten food) and careful body condition monitoring rather than guesswork ([VCA](https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/feeding-the-pregnant-cat)).

Practical points:

- Use complete and balanced commercial diets
- Make calorie increases gradually as pregnancy progresses
- Avoid random calcium or vitamin supplements unless your vet prescribes them
- Keep fresh water available at all times

## Bottom Line

Cats are usually pregnant for **63 to 65 days**, with normal variation around that average. If your cat might be pregnant, early veterinary confirmation, nutrition planning, and labor monitoring are the safest path.

If pregnancy is not desired, spaying before the first heat remains the most effective prevention strategy and also lowers serious reproductive health risks later in life.

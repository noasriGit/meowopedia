Cats lick each other as a social behavior, and many transfer that same pattern to people they trust. In behavior terms, this is close to **allogrooming**: grooming used to maintain social bonds.

## Why your cat licks you

### Social bonding and group scent
Cats in the same social group exchange scent. Licking your skin and hair can be part of the "you are mine and I am yours" routine.

### Grooming behavior redirected to people
Some cats groom owners after petting sessions the same way they groom feline housemates.

### Taste and texture
Salt on skin, lotions, or food residue can encourage brief licking episodes.

### Self-soothing
Anxious cats may lick people, blankets, or themselves to regulate arousal.

## Normal vs. excessive licking

Normal licking is brief, situational, and easy to interrupt. Excessive licking is repetitive, hard to redirect, and may appear with restlessness, hiding, or changes in sleep/appetite.

<VetTip title="If licking spikes suddenly">
Sudden behavior change deserves a medical check first. Pain, nausea, itch, and stress can all increase repetitive grooming behaviors.
</VetTip>

## What to do

- Reward calm alternatives like sitting beside you.
- End petting before overstimulation starts.
- Add enrichment: hunting games, puzzle feeders, vertical space.
- Keep a predictable feeding and play schedule.

If your cat also kneads before licking, see `behavior-making-biscuits` for the nursing-origin comfort pattern that often overlaps.

Cats vomit more often than most dogs, but frequent vomiting is still a medical sign, not a personality trait. The key question is pattern: a single, self-limited episode is very different from repeated vomiting, blood, or vomiting with low energy.

## Is It Normal to Throw Up?

Sometimes, yes. It may be relatively benign when your cat:

- has one isolated episode
- acts normal afterward
- eats and drinks normally
- has normal stool and energy

Common low-risk triggers include hairballs, eating too quickly, or a minor diet upset.

It is **not** normal when vomiting repeats, intensifies, or appears with other signs.

## Common Causes of Vomiting in Cats

| Cause | Typical clues |
|------|---|
| Hairballs | Retching, tube-shaped hair in vomit, otherwise normal behavior |
| Dietary upset | New food, table scraps, sudden diet switch |
| Gastritis or GI inflammation | Nausea, lip licking, reduced appetite |
| Parasites or infection | Vomiting with diarrhea or weight loss |
| Foreign body (string, toy) | Repeated vomiting, pain, low appetite |
| Toxins | Sudden vomiting, drooling, tremors, weakness |
| Systemic disease (including CKD) | Chronic vomiting, weight loss, thirst changes |

## Normal vs Emergency: Quick Triage

<HealthAlertBox title="Go to urgent or emergency care now">
Get immediate veterinary care if vomiting is repeated in a short period, contains blood, happens with severe lethargy, painful belly, collapse, breathing changes, or possible toxin/string ingestion.
</HealthAlertBox>

Call your regular vet the same day if vomiting lasts more than 24 hours, keeps returning over days, or comes with appetite loss, weight loss, diarrhea, or dehydration.

## Could This Be Chronic Kidney Disease?

In older cats, chronic vomiting can be one of the early clues of kidney disease. If you also notice increased thirst, larger urine clumps, or weight loss, ask your veterinarian to screen for CKD and review the `disease-ckd` article for deeper context.

## What You Can Do at Home (Short Term)

- Remove food for a short rest period only if your vet has advised this approach for your cat.
- Offer fresh water in small, frequent amounts.
- Avoid abrupt food changes.
- Track frequency, timing, vomit appearance, and triggers to share with your vet.

Do not give human anti-nausea medicines unless your veterinarian prescribes them.

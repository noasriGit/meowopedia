Many cats with feline calicivirus (FCV) recover and live normal lifespans, especially with early supportive care. Life expectancy depends on disease severity, strain type, and whether complications occur.

## Typical prognosis

For common respiratory FCV:

- Mild cases often improve in 1 to 2 weeks
- Appetite and energy return gradually
- Some cats may become chronic carriers with intermittent signs

For severe virulent systemic FCV:

- Illness can progress rapidly
- Hospitalization is often required
- Mortality risk is much higher

## Why outcomes vary

Prognosis is influenced by:

- Age (kittens are higher risk)
- Vaccination status
- Presence of oral ulcers and dehydration
- Secondary bacterial infection
- Speed of veterinary treatment

## Common signs

- Sneezing and nasal discharge
- Fever
- Mouth ulcers and drooling
- Reduced appetite
- Limping syndrome in some cases

Mouth pain can significantly reduce eating, making dehydration and hepatic lipidosis concerns if anorexia persists.

<WarningBox title="Not eating is a major risk in cats">
If a cat eats little or nothing for 24 hours, call your veterinarian promptly. Prolonged anorexia can become a medical emergency.
</WarningBox>

## Treatment approach

Treatment is supportive and tailored:

- Hydration support
- Pain control for oral ulcers
- Nutritional support
- Nasal and eye care
- Antibiotics when secondary bacterial infection is present

## Long-term management

Some recovered cats shed virus intermittently. To reduce spread:

- Keep vaccination current
- Isolate actively symptomatic cats
- Disinfect bowls and litter tools
- Minimize stress in multi-cat homes

With monitoring and early intervention during flare-ups, many FCV-positive cats maintain good quality of life for years.

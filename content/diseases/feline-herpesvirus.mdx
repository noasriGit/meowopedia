Cats get herpes through contact with infected cats, especially by sharing airspace, dishes, and surfaces contaminated with respiratory secretions. The virus is common and a major cause of upper respiratory disease in cats.

## How transmission happens

Feline herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1) spreads through:

- Sneezing droplets
- Eye and nasal discharge
- Shared bowls, litter tools, and bedding
- Close-contact housing (shelters, rescues, catteries)

Kittens and stressed cats are most likely to show obvious illness after exposure.

## What happens after infection

Many cats recover from the initial episode but retain latent virus in the body. Later, stress can reactivate shedding and symptoms.

Common reactivation triggers:

- Boarding or moving homes
- New pets or household changes
- Concurrent illness
- Immune suppression

## Symptoms of feline herpes

- Sneezing
- Nasal congestion/discharge
- Conjunctivitis
- Eye squinting or corneal irritation
- Reduced appetite from congestion

Eye complications can become serious without prompt treatment.

<HealthAlertBox title="Eye pain needs fast care">
If your cat squints, keeps an eye closed, or has cloudy corneas, seek same-day veterinary evaluation to protect vision.
</HealthAlertBox>

## Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by history and sometimes PCR testing. Treatment focuses on:

- Hydration and nutrition support
- Humidification and nasal care
- Antiviral or antibiotic therapy when indicated
- Eye-specific medications for keratitis or ulcers

## Prevention and long-term control

- Keep vaccines current (they reduce severity)
- Reduce stress during transitions
- Isolate actively sick cats from others
- Maintain clean shared resources in multi-cat homes

Many cats with FHV-1 live well with episodic care plans and early flare-up intervention.
